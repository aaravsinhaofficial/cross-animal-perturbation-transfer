\title{\bf Do individuals share a causal dynamical operator?\\
Zero-shot transfer of intervention responses between animals}

\author{CADENCE contributors}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Latent neural dynamics are strikingly similar across individuals performing the
same behaviour, and a growing family of methods aligns recordings across animals
into a shared representation. Those results are \emph{descriptive}: they show that
observed activity occupies a common geometry. They do not establish that different
individuals share a common \emph{causal} operator -- that the same physical
intervention would move each brain the same way. We formalise that stronger claim
as a prediction problem: after observing only \emph{normal, unperturbed} activity
from a new animal, predict the full time-resolved neural and behavioural response
to an intervention that animal has never received. We introduce CADENCE, a
hierarchical controlled latent dynamical system that separates a species-invariant
dynamical operator, a species-invariant causal (intervention) operator,
animal-specific observation maps and animal-specific residual dynamics; we show
that the new animal's observation map -- and hence its predicted intervention
response -- is identified from unperturbed activity alone up to the symmetry group
of the shared flow. In teacher-RNN simulations with known ground truth, zero-shot
transfer is positive when animals genuinely share dynamics ($\Delta R^2 = \TDegen$
and $\TShared$ in two such regimes, against ceilings of $\TDegenCeil$ and
$\TSharedCeil$) and negative between independently trained networks that share only
the task ($\THetero$), with every negative control below zero; with five simulated
animals per regime these estimates are imprecise, so we treat them as existence
checks rather than measurements. We then apply the framework to
\NAnimals{} mice receiving parameterised intracortical microstimulation of
somatosensory cortex during a detection task (\NSessions{} sessions, \NUnits{}
single units, \NStimTrials{} stimulation trials; DANDI:001868), holding out whole
animals and, in the strictest condition, also deleting the stimulation amplitude
from every training animal. The answer is granularity-dependent and, we argue,
biologically informative. The time-resolved behavioural response transfers
strongly to a completely new animal ($\Delta R^2 = \DetXAni$, $95\%$ CI
$[\DetXAniLo, \DetXAniHi]$, $\DetXAniFrac$ of the noise ceiling, $p = \DetP$, the floor of the
exact paired test at $n=\NSessions$),
and remains positive when the amplitude is also unseen ($\Delta R^2 =
\DetXAniUnseen$). Coarse-grained neural responses transfer partially (population
rate: $\Delta R^2 = \PopXAni$, $r = \PopXAniR$; depth bands: $\Delta R^2 =
\BandXAni$, $p = \BandP$). Single-unit responses do not transfer
($\Delta R^2 = \UnitXAni$, $p = \UnitP$) even though they are well estimated
(ceiling $\UnitXAniCeil$) and largely predictable within a session
($\Delta R^2 = \UnitIn$). We then localise that failure precisely: holding the
shared operator completely fixed and granting the held-out animal only \emph{one
scalar per unit} raises $\Delta R^2$ from $\ReadoutNone$ to $\ReadoutGain$
($p = \ReadoutGainP$), and a per-unit scalar cannot invent structure in time or
across conditions. The shared causal operator is therefore already substantially
correct; what is missing is the animal-specific map between individual neurons and
the conserved state -- and for microstimulation we show why it is missing, since the
stimulus recruits a spatially diffuse, implant-specific set of cells
($r(|\Delta z|,\Delta\text{rate}) = -0.013$). Shared neural dynamics is causal, but
only above the single-neuron scale.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Populations of neurons recorded in different individuals performing the same task
occupy remarkably similar low-dimensional manifolds, and the flow on those
manifolds looks similar too. Methods that exploit this have become standard:
local flow fields can be embedded in a common space \citep{marble}, recordings can
be stitched across sessions and subjects to yield latent dynamics that generalise
behavioural decoding \citep{candy,lfads,ndt}, and alignment techniques recover
correspondences between populations that share no neurons \citep{cca,procrustes,dsa}.

Every one of these results is a statement about \emph{observed} activity. A shared
manifold, and even a shared vector field fitted to spontaneous or task-driven
activity, is compatible with individuals whose \emph{causal} structure differs:
two dynamical systems can produce the same trajectories under the conditions
sampled and yet respond differently to the same push. The distinction matters
practically -- it decides whether a stimulation protocol calibrated in one brain
can be expected to act the same way in the next -- and conceptually, because
``shared dynamics'' is usually intended as a claim about mechanism.

We therefore ask a question that a purely observational alignment cannot answer:

\begin{quote}
\emph{After observing only normal activity from a new individual, can a model
trained on interventions in other individuals predict the full time-resolved
neural and behavioural response to an intervention that individual has never
received?}
\end{quote}

This is a strictly harder test than cross-animal decoding or manifold alignment.
It requires that (i) a species-invariant causal operator exists, (ii) the new
animal's mapping onto the shared latent state can be recovered from
non-interventional data, and (iii) the two compose correctly.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{A formal target and a model.} We define the zero-shot intervention
transfer problem and introduce CADENCE (Section~\ref{sec:model}), a hierarchical
controlled latent dynamical system that explicitly separates a shared dynamical
operator $F$, a shared state-dependent causal operator $G$, animal-specific
observation maps $H_i$ and animal-specific residual dynamics $F^{\mathrm{res}}_i$.
\item \textbf{An identifiability result, and what breaks the degeneracy.}
Proposition~\ref{prop:ident} states that with a free per-animal observation map,
transfer is identified precisely when the shared flow has trivial symmetry group,
and is otherwise ambiguous by exactly that group. Teacher-RNN simulations show
that transfer is positive when dynamics are genuinely shared, absent between
independently trained networks that share only the task, and -- informatively --
survives a
deliberately symmetric flow once observation maps are tied across animals through
shared per-unit features, because that removes the freedom the symmetry would
otherwise exploit (Section~\ref{sec:teacher}).
\item \textbf{A decomposition that localises the failure.} With the shared operator
held fixed, one scalar per unit fitted on the held-out animal lifts $\Delta R^2$ from
$\ReadoutNone$ to $\ReadoutGain$ ($\ReadoutGainPos$ sessions positive). The operator
predicts \emph{which pattern in time and across conditions} each unit shows and gets
only the per-unit amplitude wrong.
\item \textbf{A concrete mechanism for the failure at single-neuron resolution.}
Pooled over every session, a unit's response to microstimulation is essentially
uncorrelated with its distance from the stimulating contact
($r(|\Delta z|, \Delta\text{rate}) = -0.013$): low-amplitude ICMS recruits a
sparse, spatially diffuse, implant-specific set of neurons. There is therefore no
conserved neuron-level spatial rule for any model to learn, which is exactly what
we measure.
\item \textbf{A leakage-audited measurement on real multi-animal perturbation
data.} We build a reproducible benchmark from DANDI:001868 and report a
\emph{generalisation ladder} (Section~\ref{sec:icms}) that localises where
cross-animal causal transfer succeeds: strongly for behaviour, partially for
coarse-grained population activity, and not at all for single units. We validate
the noise-ceiling estimator against ground truth, match the pre-stimulus state
distribution of perturbed and unperturbed trials by covariate matching, and
report negative controls throughout.
\end{enumerate}

Our conclusion is deliberately two-sided. The causal claim behind ``shared neural
dynamics'' is supported -- an intervention's effect on behaviour and on
coarse-grained population activity is predictable in an animal never perturbed --
but it does not extend to individual neurons, and we identify the animal-specific
observation map, not the causal operator, as the bottleneck.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Cross-animal alignment.} CCA and Procrustes-based alignment, and their
nonlinear descendants, find correspondences between populations recorded in
different individuals \citep{cca,procrustes}. MARBLE embeds local flow fields into
a representation that is comparable across animals \citep{marble}; CANDY aligns
sessions and subjects and fits shared latent dynamics that transfer behavioural
decoders \citep{candy}. Dynamical similarity analysis compares systems by their
dynamics rather than their geometry \citep{dsa}. These establish representational
and dynamical alignment. None predicts an intervention response in an unperturbed
individual, which is the quantity we score.

\paragraph{Latent dynamical models of neural populations.} Sequential
autoencoders and transformer variants infer latent dynamics from spikes and
support multi-session stitching through per-session read-in/read-out matrices
\citep{lfads,ndt}. Switching and input-driven linear systems make the control
input explicit \citep{slds}. CADENCE inherits the per-session observation map idea
but adds an explicit, shared, state-dependent \emph{causal} operator and, crucially,
a protocol in which the new animal's parameters may only be fitted on unperturbed
data.

\paragraph{Perturbing neural dynamics.} Optogenetic and electrical perturbation
studies characterise how cortical dynamics absorb or amplify an imposed input
\citep{li2016,otchy}, and closed-loop and biomimetic microstimulation work asks
what a given stimulus will evoke \citep{icms_learning}. These are typically
within-animal analyses. Linear-response ideas -- using spontaneous fluctuations to
predict responses to perturbation -- have a long history in statistical physics and
have been applied to neural data \citep{fdt}; our observed-space model
(Section~\ref{sec:lr}) is a discrete-time instance, with the drive shared across
animals.

\section{Problem statement and model}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{The zero-shot intervention transfer problem}

Animal $i$ contributes trials of two kinds. \emph{Unperturbed} trials carry no
intervention. \emph{Intervention} trials carry a known, physically parameterised
stimulus $a_t$ (for microstimulation: amplitude and the cortical depth of the
stimulating contact, gated by the pulse train). We observe a
recording-specific population $y^{(i)}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{N_i}$ and a behavioural
signal $b_t$ measured in physical units that are common to all animals.

Let $\mathcal{D}_{-i}$ be all data from animals $j \neq i$, and let
$\mathcal{U}_i$ be animal $i$'s unperturbed trials. The task is to produce, for a
condition $c$ that animal $i$ has never received,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\Delta}^{(i)}_c(t) \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[y^{(i)}_t \mid a=c\right]
- \mathbb{E}\!\left[y^{(i)}_t \mid a=0\right],
\qquad\text{from } \mathcal{D}_{-i} \cup \mathcal{U}_i \text{ and } c \text{ only,}
\end{equation}
and likewise for behaviour. Note what is \emph{not} available: no intervention
trial of animal $i$, not even its pre-stimulus window.

\paragraph{Scoring.} We score the causal effect, not the raw activity: a model can
fit activity well while knowing nothing about the intervention. With
$\Delta$ the measured effect,
\begin{equation}
\Delta R^2 \;=\; 1 - \frac{\sum (\Delta - \widehat{\Delta})^2}{\sum \Delta^2},
\end{equation}
so $\Delta R^2 = 0$ is exactly the ``the intervention does nothing'' model and
$1$ is perfect. Because $\Delta$ is estimated from finitely many trials we also
compute a split-half \emph{noise ceiling}: the largest $\Delta R^2$ any model
could attain. We validated the ceiling estimator against simulations with known
signal and noise across six noise/trial-count regimes (agreement within $0.06$;
\texttt{tests/test\_metrics.py}).

\subsection{CADENCE}

For animal $i$ and recording session $s$,
\begin{align}
z_{t+1} &= z_t + \Delta t\Big[\;\underbrace{F_{\theta}(z_t,u_t)}_{\text{species-invariant dynamics}}
 \;+\; \underbrace{G_{\phi}(z_t)\,a_t}_{\text{species-invariant causal operator}}
 \;+\; \underbrace{F^{\mathrm{res}}_{i}(z_t)}_{\text{animal-specific residual}}\Big],
\label{eq:dyn}\\
y^{(i,s)}_t &\sim \mathrm{Poisson}\big(\mathrm{softplus}(C_{i,s} z_t + b_{i,s})\big),
\qquad
b_t = D_{\psi}(z_t).
\label{eq:obs}
\end{align}
The state-dependent causal operator is a low-rank multiplicative form,
$G_\phi(z)a = Ba + P\big[\tanh(W_z z)\odot(W_a a)\big]$: $B$ is the
state-independent push and the second term lets the same physical stimulus act
differently depending on where the shared state currently is. The intervention
code $a_t$ is a smooth function of \emph{physical} parameters, which is what allows
extrapolation to settings never trained on. The behavioural readout $D_\psi$ is
shared, because behaviour is measured in the same units in every animal; making it
shared is what makes behavioural transfer a nontrivial prediction rather than a
per-animal fit.

\paragraph{Observation maps from shared unit features.} A completely free $C_{i,s}$
is not recoverable from spontaneous activity (Section~\ref{sec:icms}), so we
generate it as $C_{i,s} = C_{\mathrm{shared}}(\xi_{i,s}) + C^{\mathrm{free}}_{i,s}$,
where $\xi$ collects per-unit quantities that are comparable across animals and
observable without intervening -- depth on the probe, cell class, log firing rate,
Fano factor, autocorrelation at three lags, coupling to the population -- and
$C^{\mathrm{free}}$ is a strongly penalised correction. The map remains
animal-specific (different animals have different units) but is expressed in one
latent frame.

\paragraph{Protocol.} Shared parameters $(\theta,\phi,\psi,C_{\mathrm{shared}})$
are fitted on $\mathcal{D}_{-i}$. For the held-out animal, all shared parameters
are frozen and only $(C^{\mathrm{free}}, b, F^{\mathrm{res}})$ are fitted, on
$\mathcal{U}_i$. To remove any asymmetry between training and test animals,
animal-private parameters are never updated from intervention data \emph{for any
animal}: intervention trials only ever update shared parameters. Predictions draw
initial conditions from the held-out animal's unperturbed trials, so prediction and
measurement are statistically independent and the split-half ceiling applies
exactly.

\subsection{When is transfer identified?}

\begin{definition}[Symmetry group of a flow]
For $F:\mathbb{R}^d\times\mathbb{R}^{m_u}\to\mathbb{R}^d$ let
$\mathrm{Sym}(F) = \{T \in GL(d) : T F(z,u) = F(Tz,u)\ \forall z,u\}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Transfer is identified up to $\mathrm{Sym}(F)$]\label{prop:ident}
Fix the shared operators $F$ and $G$. Suppose animal $i$'s unperturbed law is
matched exactly by parameters $(C_i, F^{\mathrm{res}}_i)$ and also by
$(C_i', F^{\mathrm{res}\prime}_i)$. Then there exists $T \in \mathrm{Sym}(F)$ with
$C_i' = C_i T^{-1}$ and $F^{\mathrm{res}\prime}_i = T F^{\mathrm{res}}_i T^{-1}$,
and the two parameterisations predict intervention responses that differ by
$C_i T^{-1} \big(T G(Tz)a - G(z)a\big)$. Consequently: if
$\mathrm{Sym}(F)=\{I\}$ the predicted intervention response is unique, and if
$\mathrm{Sym}(F)$ is nontrivial the prediction is ambiguous by exactly that group
unless $G$ is equivariant under it.
\end{proposition}

\noindent The proof is immediate from substituting $z \mapsto Tz$ into
Eq.~\ref{eq:dyn}--\ref{eq:obs} and requiring the unperturbed law to be preserved.
Two consequences drive the rest of the paper. First, transfer needs the shared flow
to be \emph{asymmetric} -- rich, heterogeneous dynamics help, and degenerate
(isotropic, rotationally symmetric) dynamics are fatal. Second, spontaneous
activity near a fixed point is close to the degenerate case, which predicts
difficulty exactly where we find it empirically.

\subsection{A linear-response formulation in the observed space}\label{sec:lr}

Proposition~\ref{prop:ident} suggests an alternative that avoids latent frames
altogether. Estimate each animal's propagator directly from its own unperturbed
activity and share only the \emph{drive}:
\begin{equation}
y_{t+1} - \mu_i = A_i\,(y_t - \mu_i) + u^{(i)}_t,\qquad
u^{(i)}_t(n) = \sum_{j,l,\beta}\theta_{j l \beta}\,
\psi_l(\mathrm{amp})\,B_\beta(t)\,
e^{-\left(\frac{\mathrm{depth}_n - d}{\sigma_j}\right)^2},
\label{eq:lr}
\end{equation}
so that $\Delta_i(t) = \sum_{k\le t} A_i^{k} u^{(i)}_{t-k}$: the animal's own
impulse response convolved with a species-invariant drive. $A_i$ and $\mu_i$ come
from unperturbed trials only; $\theta$ is fitted on other animals and, because
$\Delta$ is linear in $\theta$, in closed form. Equation~\ref{eq:lr} is a
discrete-time fluctuation--response statement: spontaneous propagation predicts the
response to a perturbation. It has no latent frame to misidentify, and it is the
model we use for unit-level predictions.

\section{Teacher-RNN benchmark with known ground truth}\label{sec:teacher}

We build synthetic animals whose shared operator, causal operator, observation
maps, residual dynamics and conserved/idiosyncratic intervention directions are
all known. Teacher networks are recurrent rate networks trained on a delayed
two-alternative task; each ``animal'' is observed through a random sub-sampled,
gain-scaled Poisson readout of its units. Three regimes isolate the assumptions.

\begin{description}\itemsep2pt
\item[shared] One teacher defines the species operator; animals differ by a
small low-rank perturbation of the recurrent weights (the ground-truth residual)
and by their observation maps. A shared causal operator genuinely exists.
\item[heterogeneous] Each animal is an \emph{independently trained} network. No
parameters are shared -- only the task. Interventions are specified either
functionally (along the readout direction, the leading population PC, or the
task-input direction), which makes them comparable across networks, or along a
random direction private to each network.
\item[degenerate] A teacher whose flow is invariant to rotations of
two-dimensional isotropic blocks, so $\mathrm{Sym}(F)$ is large and, with a free
observation map, Proposition~\ref{prop:ident} says the observation map is
unidentifiable.
\end{description}

Table~\ref{tab:teacher} reports leave-one-animal-out transfer.

\paragraph{Transfer is positive where dynamics are genuinely shared.} In the
degenerate regime, where every animal inherits the same teacher exactly,
zero-shot transfer is positive in $\TDegenPos$ folds ($\Delta R^2 = \TDegen$,
ceiling $\TDegenCeil$; behaviour $\TDegenBeh$). In the shared regime, where animals
additionally carry an animal-specific low-rank perturbation of the recurrent
weights, the mean is positive but variable across folds
($\Delta R^2 = \TShared$, $\TSharedPos$ folds above zero, behaviour $\TSharedBeh$).
Negative controls are clearly below zero in both (permuted unit identities
$\TSharedCtrlPerm$, scrambled intervention labels $\TSharedCtrlScr$ in the shared
regime). With only five simulated animals per regime the confidence intervals are
wide -- this benchmark is an existence check that the protocol can work at all, not
a precise measurement; the well-powered numbers in this paper are the biological
ones in Section~\ref{sec:icms}, which rest on \NSessions{} sessions.

\paragraph{Independently trained networks do not transfer.} In the heterogeneous
regime $\Delta R^2 = \THetero$ with only $\THeteroPos$ folds above zero, while the
oracle reaches $\THeteroOracle$. Networks that solve the same task with unrelated
weights therefore do not share a causal operator that our model can recover at this
scale -- even though the same model, given the held-out network's intervention
trials, fits it well. Sharing a task is not sharing an operator.

\paragraph{The oracle gap is the same everywhere.} Across all three regimes the
oracle sits far above zero-shot ($\TSharedOracle$, $\THeteroOracle$, $\TDegenOracle$
versus $\TShared$, $\THetero$, $\TDegen$). The model can express these intervention
responses; what limits zero-shot transfer is calibrating the held-out animal. That
is the same conclusion the biological data force, and Section~\ref{sec:whyhard}
quantifies it there.

\paragraph{Symmetry is broken by tying observation maps, not by the data.} The
degenerate teacher still transfers ($\Delta R^2 = \TDegen$). This is not a
contradiction, and it is instructive. Proposition~\ref{prop:ident} assumes a
\emph{free} observation map per animal. Generating $C_i$ from a shared function of
per-unit features removes exactly that freedom: a rotation $T$ that would leave the
unperturbed law invariant is no longer available, because $C_i$ is not free to
absorb it. The symmetry is broken by the model's parameterisation rather than by
the data. Two consequences follow, and both matter for the real data: tying
observation maps across animals is what makes transfer possible at all, and the
transfer is then only as good as the features are informative about a unit's causal
coupling.

\section{Cross-animal intervention transfer in mouse somatosensory cortex}\label{sec:icms}

\subsection{Benchmark}

We use DANDI:001868 \citep{icms_learning}: \NAnimals{} chronically implanted mice
trained to report intracortical microstimulation of S1-trunk by turning a wheel.
The intervention is fully parameterised -- amplitude \AmpMin--\AmpMax\,\textmu A
(\NAmps{} levels) at 100\,Hz, 70 biphasic pulses, delivered on a chosen contact of
a 32-channel linear probe -- so ``an intervention never seen in training'' is well
defined: a new amplitude, a new contact depth, or both. After quality control we
retain \NSessions{} sessions from \NAnimals{} mice, \NUnits{} single units,
\NStimTrials{} stimulation trials and \NUnpTrials{} unperturbed windows, binned at
\BinMs\,ms over $[-0.5, +1.5]$\,s around stimulation onset (median \MedConds{}
amplitude$\times$contact conditions per session, \MinUnits--\MaxUnits{} units).

\paragraph{Unperturbed data and covariate matching.} Unperturbed data come from
$0$\,\textmu A catch trials and from inter-trial windows drawn from the gaps
between consecutive task trials. Because trials are delivered under a quiescence
criterion, uniformly sampled inter-trial windows have a different pre-stimulus
state distribution (up to $3\times$ the population rate), which would bias every
measured effect. We therefore match candidate windows to the stimulation trials on
a joint quantile grid of pre-stimulus population rate and wheel speed, followed by
a mean-matching trim, and we \emph{exclude} any session whose residual mismatch
exceeds $1.25\times$. The released audit reports \NAuditProblems{} remaining
problems across all sessions, and the pipeline is bit-for-bit reproducible.

\paragraph{Effects are large and well estimated.} Stimulation roughly doubles the
population rate for the duration of the train, with a graded (and, above
$\sim$5\,\textmu A, saturating) dependence on amplitude and a post-offset
undershoot (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}). It is, however, \emph{not} spatially
organised around the stimulating contact -- a point we return to in
Section~\ref{sec:whyhard}. Median split-half ceilings are $\CeilUnitMed$ for units
and $\CeilBehMed$ for behaviour, so the measurements are not the limiting factor.

\subsection{A generalisation ladder}

We score the same trials at five granularities and under four generalisation
levels (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}). \emph{In-sample} fits and tests on the same
session and measures how predictable the response is at all. \emph{New session}
tests on a held-out session of the same animal -- new units, same individual.
\emph{New animal} is leave-one-animal-out. \emph{New animal + unseen amplitude}
additionally deletes the evaluated amplitude from every training animal, so both
the individual and the intervention setting are unseen.

\paragraph{Behaviour transfers.} The time-resolved probability that the animal has
reported detection by time $t$ -- a genuine behavioural response curve with a
strong dose dependence -- is predicted in a completely new animal at
$\Delta R^2 = \DetXAni$ $[\DetXAniLo, \DetXAniHi]$, $r = \DetXAniR$, which is
$\DetXAniFrac$ of the noise ceiling ($\DetXAniCeil$); \DetXAniPos{} sessions and
\DetNAnimalsPos{} animals are above zero (per animal: \DetPerAnimalList).
A paired permutation test against the no-effect model gives $p = \DetP$ --- the
smallest value the exact test can return with $n = \NSessions$ paired sessions,
i.e.\ every session favoured the model --- and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test gives
$p = \DetPW$. With the amplitude also deleted from every training
animal, prediction remains positive ($\Delta R^2 = \DetXAniUnseen$,
$r = \DetXAniUnseenR$, \DetXAniUnseenPos{} sessions above zero). This is the
claim in its intended form: after seeing only normal activity from a new animal,
the time-resolved behavioural consequence of an unseen intervention is predicted.

\paragraph{Coarse-grained neural activity transfers partially.} The
population-mean response transfers at $\Delta R^2 = \PopXAni$ with a high shape
correlation ($r = \PopXAniR$): the time course and dose dependence are conserved,
while the overall gain varies between animals (across-animal coefficient of
variation of effect magnitude $\approx 0.25$), which is what limits $\Delta R^2$.
Depth-band responses give $\Delta R^2 = \BandXAni$
$[\BandXAniLo, \BandXAniHi]$, $p = \BandP$.

\paragraph{Single units do not transfer.} At single-unit resolution
$\Delta R^2 = \UnitXAni$ $[\UnitXAniLo, \UnitXAniHi]$, indistinguishable from the
no-effect model ($p = \UnitP$), despite a ceiling of $\UnitXAniCeil$. The
correlation is small but positive ($r = \UnitXAniR$), so there is \emph{some}
conserved structure, just not enough: since $\Delta R^2 \approx 2r - 1$ for a
correctly scaled prediction, $r > 0.5$ is required merely to beat the null.
This is not a failure of a particular model. Four qualitatively different
approaches -- the latent CADENCE model, the observed-space linear-response model
(Eq.~\ref{eq:lr}), a physical-feature encoding model, and a manifold-alignment
group-average -- all plateau at $r \approx 0.28$. Nor is it a failure of the data:
the same models reach $\Delta R^2 = \UnitIn$ within a session and
$\Delta R^2 = \UnitXSess$ on a held-out session of the same animal. Adding
spontaneous fluctuation--response features raises within-session accuracy
substantially ($\Delta R^2$ $\FRWithin$, $r = \FRWithinR$) without improving
cross-animal transfer ($\FRXAni$), which localises the problem precisely: the
missing quantity is the correspondence between an individual neuron and the
conserved latent state, and that correspondence is not recoverable from
spontaneous activity in $\approx 20$ simultaneously recorded units.

\subsection{Why single units are hard}\label{sec:whyhard}

Two independent lines of evidence localise the failure, and neither implicates the
causal operator.

\paragraph{The readout, not the operator.} We can localise the missing ingredient to
a handful of numbers. Hold the shared drive \emph{completely fixed} at the value
fitted on the other animals, and grant on the held-out animal only a per-unit
rescaling of the predicted effect, fitted on its intervention trials. This is not a
prediction -- it uses data the protocol forbids -- but it is a decomposition, and a
tightly constrained one: a per-unit scalar cannot invent structure in time or across
conditions, because each unit's predicted time course and dose dependence are fixed
by the shared operator. One scalar per unit takes $\Delta R^2$ from
$\ReadoutNone$ to $\ReadoutGain$ (paired difference $\ReadoutGainDiff$,
$p = \ReadoutGainP$; $\ReadoutGainPos$ sessions and all \NAnimals{} animals
positive; Table~\ref{tab:oracle}). Adding a per-unit offset gives
$\ReadoutGainOff$, and a per-unit gain plus a single temporal profile shared by all
units gives $\ReadoutTime$.

The shared causal operator is therefore already substantially correct: it predicts
\emph{which pattern in time and across conditions} each unit will show, and gets the
per-unit amplitude wrong. Proposition~\ref{prop:ident} explains why that amplitude is
not recoverable from spontaneous activity: near a fixed point the flow is weak and
close to isotropic, so the freedom left in the observation map is large. The teacher
experiments show that tying observation maps through shared per-unit features can
remove that freedom entirely -- but only if the features carry information about a
unit's causal coupling. In this preparation they do not, for the reason below.

\label{tab:oracle}
\begin{tabular}{llcc}

\paragraph{In this preparation, they cannot.} Pooled over all \NSessions{} sessions
and every condition, a unit's response is essentially uncorrelated with its
distance from the stimulating contact ($r(|\Delta z|, \Delta\text{rate}) = -0.013$;
Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}b): there is no peak at the electrode and no monotone falloff,
only a broad, spatially diffuse elevation extending over more than a millimetre in
both directions. This is the signature of low-amplitude ICMS activating axons of
passage -- a sparse, distributed and implant-specific set of cells rather than a
dense local sphere -- which is what direct imaging of microstimulation-evoked
activity reports \citep{histed}. Which particular neurons are recruited is then a property of
that individual electrode placement, not of the species. No shared function of
observable unit features can predict it, which is precisely the ceiling we hit.
Consistently, the neural dose--response saturates and in several animals reverses
above $\sim$5\,\textmu A (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}c) while the behavioural response
keeps growing: the aggregate drive and its behavioural consequence are conserved,
the identity of the driven cells is not.

\section{Discussion}

Cross-animal similarity of neural dynamics is real, and our results say it is more
than descriptive: an intervention's time-resolved effect on behaviour, and to a
useful extent on coarse-grained population activity, can be predicted in an
individual that has never been perturbed, and even for a stimulus setting absent
from the entire training cohort. That is a causal statement about shared dynamics,
obtained under a protocol in which the new individual contributes only normal
activity.

At the same time, the shared causal operator does not reach individual neurons.
We think this is the biologically interesting part of the answer. What is conserved
across individuals is an operator on a coarse-grained state -- how much drive a
given stimulus injects, how long it lasts, how it decays, what behaviour it
produces -- and not the assignment of that state to particular cells. For
microstimulation the reason is concrete: the stimulus recruits a sparse, spatially
diffuse set of neurons whose identity is a property of the individual electrode
placement, so there is nothing species-level to learn at that resolution. The
teacher experiments show the complementary point -- that tying observation maps
across animals through shared per-unit features is what makes any transfer possible,
and that transfer is then bounded by how much those features say about a unit's
causal coupling.

A practical reading: a stimulation protocol calibrated in one animal can be
expected to reproduce its behavioural and population-level effect in the next, and
should not be expected to reproduce its effect on any particular cell.

\paragraph{Limitations.} (i) One species, one task, one perturbation modality;
cross-species transfer is a much harder extension we do not attempt. (ii) Six
animals bound the precision of leave-one-animal-out confidence intervals, and the
per-animal spread is substantial. (iii) Simultaneously recorded populations are
small ($\MinUnits$--$\MaxUnits$ units); denser recordings would test directly
whether the observation map becomes identifiable with more neurons, which our
diagnosis predicts. (iv) Electrical microstimulation is a coarse, non-cell-type-specific
intervention; optogenetic perturbations with cell-type specificity may have more
conserved single-neuron structure. (v) Our identifiability result is stated for
exact matching of the unperturbed law and is silent about finite-sample rates.

\paragraph{What would change the conclusion.} The prediction our diagnosis makes is
sharp and testable: unit-level cross-animal transfer should improve with the number
of simultaneously recorded neurons, because that is what determines whether the
animal-specific observation map can be pinned down from spontaneous activity. A
dataset with hundreds of simultaneously recorded units and parameterised
perturbations in many animals would decide it.
